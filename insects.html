Outdoor Acoustic Insect Detection Device

Languages: Python
In collaboration with Nicolas Kaye, Edward Sicoe, Armaan Osahan, and Martin Thomas Moreno.

Video edited by Nicolas Kaye

This was an academic project spanning the entire winter semester. In a team with 4 peers, we were approached by a real client, Oak Street Garden, and given a real issue to solve: We were to design a device to aid the garden in understanding what pollinators were present and when. Over 4 months, we had produced a weather-proof listening device, along with external software to analyze the audio, and a website to provide a visual summary. Throughout the project, I was primarily responsible for designing the audio analysis software.

Hardware

- ESP32
- INMP441 microphone
- SD Card
- 10,000 mAH battery
- PLA filament

Device Functions

The device itself was designed to remain outdoors for 8 hours, roughly a full work day for the client. PSA filament was used for the exterior, as it offered acceptable environmental resistance while being readily available. Construction of the device was also sealed as tightly as possible, and battery life was an estimated 47 hours.

The device listens using its INMP441 microphone, a small mic that is responsive to relevant insect frequencies. These recordings are saved to an on-board SD card that can be removed for audio analysis.

Software

This was my area of responsibility. To detect insect sounds, the Python library openSoundscape was used. From Kitzes Lab at the University of Pittsburg, openSoundscape offers several tools to make machine learning models specifically for animal noise recognition.

Convolutonal Neural Networks

Specifically, openSoundscape creates convolutional neural networks (CNN), a type of classifier (models designed to classify data.) These CNN's identify specific sounds by examining the spectrogram (visual representation of audio), and recognizing which parts of the spectrogram resemble what it was trained on. openSoundscape lists example uses for these models as bird or frog calls. However, insect noises would have been a novel application and more challenging. While some insects like crickets or cicadas are notoriously loud, we were looking for pollinators like bees and wasps. Their buzzes are much more quiet. Our training ensured our model would effectively identify these sounds.

Training

Training data for our model was primarily sourced from InsectSound1000, a dataset containing 95.6 GB-worth of audio from 12 insect species. This dataset offered a plethora of high quality clips specifically designed for audio recognition tasks like this. It was also one of the only suitable datasets that was freely available, as budget was a major consideration. That being said, it wasn't perfect. InsectSound1000 is geared towards European insects, meaning many were not relevant to Kingston, Ontario. Still, it was decided that Bombus terrestris (buff-tailed bumblebee, extant in Europe) would be an acceptable stand-in for North American bees. Other North American insects were included, mainly pests such as gnats and stink bugs. Insects not present in North America comprised the negative data for training and testing. In total, over 12,000 audio clips were used, totalling 8 GB. For a rough prototype, that was enough to make an effective model.

Testing and Results

The effectiveness for the model was determined using its F1 score. Simply put, it is a single number that quantifies its accuracy, i.e. how many positive predictions were correct out of all positive predictions, and how many positive predictions were correct out of all actual positives. This score ranges from 0, indicating total failure, to 1, meaning it classified everything correctly.

Over 8200 audio clips were used in testing, equivalent to 5.2 GB. Of course, the testing data was seperate from the training set. The resulting F1 score was consistently around 0.90, indicating a highly effective model at identifying Bombus terrestris and certain pests.

It was originally intended to test the device and software in an outdoor environment. Unfortunately, this project took place during the winter term. The weather during this time was not conducive to insect activity.

Limitations

Improvements to the device's software would prioritize more data, and more relevant data. While InsectSound1000 provides vast amounts of high quality data, for Oak Street Garden, it is more relevant to target specifically North American pollinators. Unfortunately, such datasets are difficult to obtain; For example, most datasets on honeybees are concerned with hive sounds. This would be irrelevant to our client, interested in pollinator population and not hives specifically.

Also, it was intended to run the analysis software on the device itself. However, due to a quirk with openSoundscape, it must be run on a Linux device (or virtual machine). This seems to be a common limitation with Python machine learning libraries. Future iterations of the device would either use a different microcontroller capable of running Linux, or use an ML library that does not have such restrictions.

Website

While the website was not my responsibility, it ties in closely with my analysis software. When the machine learning model analyses a set of recordings, it outputs its predictions to a CSV and JSON file (to give my web development peers flexibility). The files are then uploaded to the website, which is hosted on a different device, and it provides an intuitive, visual summary of the analysis.
